Approaches to Artificial Intelligence
The four approaches to AI represent different perspectives or
methodologies for developing intelligent systems.
Humanly:
Emulating Human Intelligence: Humanly in AI
refers to designing systems that imitate or replicate
human-like intelligence, behavior, and capabilities.
Examples: Developing AI chatbots that can
engage in conversations using natural language
understanding, or creating robots that exhibit
emotions and social behaviors similar to humans.
Rationally:
Optimizing Decision-Making: Rationally in AI
means making decisions that are logical, optimal,
or appropriate given the available information and
objectives.
Examples: Designing self-driving cars that
navigate roads safely and efficiently by analyzing
sensor data and making decisions to minimize
risks and reach their destinations in the most
effective way.
Acting humanly: The Turing Test approach
Acting Humanly:
What it is: Making AI systems act or interact in ways that seem
human-like.
Example: Developing virtual assistants like Siri or Alexa that can
understand natural language commands and respond in a
conversational manner.
The Turing Test, proposed by Alan Turing in 1950, is a benchmark
for determining a machine's ability to exhibit human-like
intelligence.
In this approach to AI, known as 'Acting Humanly,' the focus is on
creating computer systems that can interact with humans in a way
that is indistinguishable from human interaction.
1.Acting humanly: The Turing Test approach
❑ The test works by having
a human interrogator
engage in a conversation
with both a human and a
computer, without
knowing which is which.
❑ If the interrogator cannot
reliably determine which
responses come from the
computer, then the
computer is said to have
passed the Turing Test.
1.Acting humanly: The Turing Test approach
To accomplish this, the computer must possess several key capabilities: KBneeded, automated reasoning is required . Apart from that:
1.Natural Language Processing (NLP): The ability to understand and generate
human language naturally is crucial for effective communication.
2.Knowledge Representation: The computer must be able to store and access
knowledge, enabling it to answer questions and engage in meaningful dialogue.
3.Automated Reasoning: The capacity to use stored information to draw logical
conclusions and respond appropriately to queries.
4.Machine Learning: Adaptability is essential for the computer to learn from
interactions and improve its performance over time.
5.Computer Vision: To pass the 'Total Turing Test,' the computer must be able to
perceive objects visually, allowing it to interact with the physical world.
6.Robotics: Additionally, the computer needs the capability to manipulate objects
and move around physically, completing the requirements for the 'Total Turing
Test.'
2. Thinking Humanly: The Cognitive Modeling Approach
Understanding human thought processes is essential for creating AI systems that can think and
behave like humans.
1.Methods of Understanding Human Cognition:
1. Introspection: Attempting to grasp our own thoughts and mental processes as they
occur.
2. Psychological Experiments: Observing individuals in various tasks and situations to
study how they think and make decisions.
3. Brain Imaging: Utilizing technologies such as brain imaging to observe brain activity
during cognitive tasks, providing insights into neural mechanisms underlying
cognition.
2.Expressing Human Cognition as Computer Programs:
1. Once researchers have developed a sufficiently precise theory of human cognition,
they can translate it into computer programs.
2. These programs attempt to replicate the cognitive processes observed in humans, such
as problem-solving, decision-making, and language understanding.
3.Validating Cognitive Models:
1. Researchers compare the behavior of these AI systems with human behavior to
validate the cognitive models.
2. For example, pioneers like Allen Newell and Herbert Simon developed the "General
Problem Solver" (GPS) and compared its problem-solving steps with those of human
subjects, providing evidence for shared cognitive mechanisms.
3. Thinking rationally: The “laws of thought” approach
The "Laws of Thought" approach, rooted in the work of Greek philosopher
Aristotle, seeks to formalize irrefutable reasoning processes through logic.
Aristotle's syllogisms, such as "Socrates is a man; all men are mortal; therefore,
Socrates is mortal," provided foundational patterns for constructing valid
arguments. The "Laws of Thought" approach is all about using formal rules of reasoning to solve
problems. It's like following a set of rules to reach the right answer every time, just like
in a game with strict rules.
Key Points:
1.Foundation in Logic: This approach is based on using rules of logic, like those
created by the ancient Greek philosopher Aristotle.
2.Logic in Artificial Intelligence: People have made computer programs that can follow
these logical rules to solve problems.
3.Challenges: Sometimes, it's hard to turn real-life situations into these logical rules.
Also, these programs can get stuck if they have too much to think about, like trying to
solve a problem with too many pieces.
4. Acting rationally: The rational agent approach
❑ In the world of artificial intelligence, An agent is something that acts
autonomously to achieve its goals.
❑ A rational agent, in particular, is one that makes decisions to achieve the best
outcome or, when faced with uncertainty, the best expected outcome.
Example:
Let's think about a robot in a factory. Its job is to assemble cars. To do this, it has to
do many things:
•Perceive its Environment: It needs to see where the car parts are and understand
how to pick them up.
•Operate Autonomously: It works on its own without needing someone to tell it
what to do all the time.
•Persist Over Time: It works for a long time without getting tired or needing
breaks.
•Adapt to Change: If there's a problem, like a part missing, it finds a solution to
keep working.
•Create and Pursue Goals: Its main goal is to assemble cars correctly and
efficiently.

Rationality
Understanding Rationality:
In the realm of artificial intelligence, rationality refers to the ability of an
agent to make decisions that are expected to achieve its goals or objectives
effectively. Essentially, it's about doing the right thing given the available
information and resources.
Rationality in artificial intelligence depends on four key factors:
1.Performance Measure: This defines the criterion of success for the
agent.
2.Prior Knowledge: The agent's understanding of the environment before
taking any action.
3.Available Actions: The actions that the agent can perform in its
environment.
4.Percept Sequence: The agent's history of sensory inputs up to the
present moment.
Applying the concept of
rationality to the vacuum cleaner
agent
Performance Measure:
In the case of the vacuum cleaner agent, the performance measure could be
defined as the total number of clean squares over a certain period of time.
For example, the agent earns one point for each clean square at each time
step. The objective is to maximize the number of clean squares to achieve a
high score.
Prior Knowledge:
Before the vacuum cleaner starts operating, it may have some prior
knowledge about the environment. This includes knowing the layout of the
space it needs to clean (such as the arrangement of squares A and B),
understanding its own capabilities and limitations, and being aware of the
actions it can take.
Applying the concept of
rationality to the vacuum cleaner
agent
Available Actions:
The vacuum cleaner agent has a limited set of actions it can perform:
moving left, moving right, or sucking up dirt. These actions are determined
by the agent's programming and are based on its understanding of the
environment.
Percept Sequence:
As the vacuum cleaner moves around the environment, it continuously
perceives its current location and whether that location contains dirt. This
sequence of sensory inputs forms the percept sequence, which informs
the agent's decision-making process.
Rationality and Adaptability
Rationality of the Vacuum Cleaner Agent:
Under these circumstances, the vacuum cleaner agent is rational. Its
expected performance is as high as or higher than any other agent's in the
same environment.
Adaptability of Rationality:
However, the same agent may be irrational under different conditions.
For instance, if all dirt is cleaned, the agent may oscillate needlessly,
resulting in poor performance. In such cases, a better agent would adjust
its actions based on the current environment and performance measure.
For instance, if it perceives that all squares are clean, it may stop moving
and remain idle until new dirt appears.
THE NATURE OF
ENVIRONMENTS
❑ In AI, we're diving into the world of rational agents, which are essentially
problem solvers.
❑ Task environments, which are essentially the “problems” to which
rational agents are the “solutions.”
❑ These environments essentially define the problems the agents will solve.
❑ The design of intelligent agents process involves specifying the task
environment, which includes identifying the performance measure, the
environment itself, and the agent's actuators and sensors.
❑ This description is often abbreviated as PEAS, standing for
Performance, Environment, Actuators, and Sensors.
Understanding PEAS
Description
PEAS is like a blueprint for designing intelligent agents. Let's break it down
using the example of an automated taxi driver:
Performance
•Definition: This describes what success looks like for the agent. It's like
setting the goals or objectives.
•Example: For our automated taxi driver, the performance measure includes
objectives like reaching the destination safely, minimizing fuel consumption,
obeying traffic laws, and maximizing profits.
Environment
•Definition: This encompasses everything the agent interacts with or
perceives.
•Example: The taxi's environment includes roads, traffic, pedestrians, and
passengers. It's the world the taxi operates in.
Understanding PEAS
Description
Actuators
•Definition: Actuators are the tools or mechanisms the agent uses to
influence the environment.
•Example: For our taxi driver, actuators include the steering wheel,
accelerator, brakes, turn signals, and communication devices like a horn or
display screen.
Sensors
•Definition: Sensors gather information from the environment, allowing the
agent to perceive and understand its surroundings.
•Example: The taxi's sensors include cameras for vision, sonar or radar for
detecting other vehicles, GPS for navigation, speedometer, and various
engine sensors to monitor the vehicle's condition.
Bringing it All Together: Automated Taxi
Driver Example
•Performance: The taxi aims for safe, fast, and legal trips while maximizing
passenger comfort and profits.
•Environment: It operates on roads with other vehicles, pedestrians, and
potential passengers.
•Actuators: The taxi controls its movement using steering, acceleration,
braking, and communicates with passengers through displays or voice.
•Sensors: It perceives its surroundings through cameras, sonar, GPS, and
various vehicle sensors.
By defining the PEAS description, we provide a clear understanding of the
agent's objectives, its operating environment, the tools it has to interact with the
world, and the information it gathers to make decisions. This framework helps
in designing effective and efficient intelligent agents for various tasks and
environments.

MODEL-BASED REFLEX AGENT
PROGRAM
A Model-Based Reflex Agent is an enhanced version of a simple reflex
agent. It maintains an internal state that reflects some unobservable aspects of
the current world state. This internal state is updated based on percepts and a
model of how the world changes. The term "model-based" comes from the fact
that the agent uses a model of the world to make decisions.
Key Components
1.Internal State: Keeps track of information that cannot be directly observed
but is necessary for making informed decisions.
2.World Model: Represents how the world works and how the agent's actions
affect the world.
Why is it Called a Model-Based Reflex Agent?
It is called "model-based" because the agent uses an internal model to
keep track of the world state, unlike a simple reflex agent, which only
relies on the current percepts. The model helps the agent understand and
predict the effects of its actions.
Model-based reflex agent

► Model: It is knowledge about "how things
